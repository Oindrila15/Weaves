# 4.1 and 4.2 Explicit criteria and few-shot (Domain 4, 20% of the exam)

Code: `study/CCAR-F/day3/ch1_explicit_criteria.py` and `ch2_few_shot.py` (real output saved at the bottom of each).

## 4.1 Explicit criteria
- Claude only knows what is in the prompt, fills gaps with its best guess, and can vary between runs.
- Vague words ("be conservative", "only high confidence") have no definition and no threshold. They do not improve precision.
- A good prompt has four parts: the job; what to include (with examples); what to skip (with examples); the output format, including what to say when nothing is found.
- Test for a good rule: two people would apply it the same way.
- **False positive** = flagged but should not have been. **False negative** = missed. Many false positives destroy reviewer trust, so turn off a noisy category and fix it separately.
- Severity levels need written definitions and one example each.
- `stop_reason` says why Claude stopped. `max_tokens` means cut off; thinking uses the same budget, so a tiny limit can give an empty answer.
- Our run: vague prompt gave three different answers, flagged sales pressure and missed the certification claim. The explicit prompt gave the same correct set every time.

## 4.2 Few-shot
- In-context learning: Claude picks up a pattern from examples in the prompt. Zero-shot = none, one-shot = one, few-shot = 2 to 4 for ambiguous cases.
- Best for house conventions, output format, judgement boundaries and "not stated so null" cases.
- Good examples: ambiguous cases, with the reason, varied, include a "nothing found" case, consistent, labelled.
- Risks: copying, teaching a mistake, token cost, over-fitting.
- Rules say the standard; examples show it at the edges. Use both.
- Our run: easy cases needed no examples. The midpoint rule for ranges worked only with examples, and carried over to an unseen range because the reason was included.
